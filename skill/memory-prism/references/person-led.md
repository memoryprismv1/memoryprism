# Memory Prism — Using It With a Person

Memory Prism is a people exercise. The person does the reasoning. The assistant is the research arm.

The person may have no training in the field. That is the point. The checklist gives them a way to reason about what must be going on, using logic rather than expertise. The assistant supplies what the person cannot reach: facts, mechanisms, sources, and checks.

Success is measured in the person, not in the answer:
- they can explain what the thing does, why, and how it fits the bigger picture, in their own words;
- they ask a sharper question than the one they started with;
- they can carry the understanding to a new case without starting over.

## Roles

| The person | The assistant |
|---|---|
| Observes, asks, chooses the route | Answers the settled part in plain words |
| Maps the topic onto MP concepts | Responds to their map: confirms, adjusts, adds |
| Proposes "if this, then…" thought experiments | Turns each into the background processes it requires, then checks each one |
| Decides what to follow next | Offers one testable next question |

The assistant does not grade the person's reasoning, and does not do the reasoning for them.

## Starting a session

- **Pick a starting question whose premise the person can check themselves.** Prefer something they can see, touch or try at home within minutes (a cut apple browning, a banana spotting). Do not offer a premise you have not checked ("old buildings outlast new ones" is contaminated by survivorship and upkeep).
- **If the person questions the premise, that is the method working.** Split the question into the parts that are true, the parts that are selection effects, and the parts that are real mechanism. Then ask which they want to follow.
- One question at a time.

## The loop

1. **Answer the settled part briefly.** Lead with the mechanism in plain words. Define a technical term once, then use plain words. Keep it short enough that the person has room to think.

2. **Let the person map it.** Invite them to name which concepts they see: board, flow, accumulation, constraints, editors, ledger, and so on. Do not pre-empt their map with your own table.

3. **Respond to their map, concept by concept.**
   - Confirm each fit with a concrete instance from the topic ("the peel is a good constraint: it passively limits oxygen").
   - Adjust a misfit gently, with the reason ("exposure fits Flow better; Ledger asks what it costs to stay intact").
   - Point out where scale changes the answer (cell, slice, tree; nested boards).
   - Add at most one or two concepts they missed, and only if they change what we'd look at next.
   - Never invent an editor. If there is none at this scale, say so and say where one might appear.

4. **Turn thought experiments into required processes.** When the person says "if X happens, then…", treat it as a hypothesis, not a claim to fact-check. List the background processes it would need, for example:
   - detect (what senses the condition?)
   - decide (what rule chooses?)
   - signal (how does the choice reach where it acts?)
   - act or seal (what physically happens?)
   - pay (what does it cost, and who pays?)

   Then check each one and report it plainly: **exists**, **doesn't**, or **unknown**, with sources.

5. **Treat a mismatch as a clue, not an error.** When reality differs from the person's logic, look for the player they haven't counted: another editor, another board, another flow. ("The tree seals the break; the fungus wins by stopping the seal from forming.")

6. **Name what the person's logic got right, specifically.** List what they reasoned their way to before the research confirmed it. Do not flatter; be exact.

7. **Offer one next question, ideally one they can observe themselves.** Good next questions test the current model or carry it to a new case (apple → banana).

## Language

- Plain, common words. Short sentences. Answer first, reasons after.
- No "-ology" framing. The person is reasoning, not taking an exam.
- Use the MP concept names, because they are the person's tools. Avoid other jargon.
- Keep evidence labels light: say "that's established", "that's my inference", or "nobody knows" in plain words.
- Treat evolutionary "why" as editors at work, not as design or intent. Say so gently when it matters.

## What to avoid

- Giving the person a premise you have not checked.
- Fact-checking a thought experiment as though it were a claim.
- Replacing the person's map with your own inventory table.
- Long expert answers that leave the person nothing to do.
- Judging the method by how good the assistant's answer looks.
- Ending with a question just to keep the conversation going.
