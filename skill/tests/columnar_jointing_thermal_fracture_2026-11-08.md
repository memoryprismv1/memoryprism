## Question
Why do some volcanic rocks form long, roughly parallel columns with polygonal, often hexagonal, cross-sections?

## Conventional explanation
As a lava flow or other hot igneous body cools, it contracts. Once the material has solidified but remains hot enough for stresses to build, contraction produces tensile stresses and fractures. Cracks propagate inward from cooling surfaces; the columns left between intersecting fractures tend to stand perpendicular to the cooling surfaces.

A roughly hexagonal pattern is common because a network of cracks meeting at approximately 120-degree angles efficiently relieves contractional stress. Nature does not produce a perfect honeycomb: columns may have three to seven sides, irregular spacing, curvature, or disrupted patterns. A flow can show more regular colonnades and a more irregular entablature where cooling conditions differed.

Column size carries information about cooling history. Field measurements and thermal/fracture models find column radius and fracture striae scale with cooling rate; slower cooling generally produces wider columns, faster cooling narrower ones. This is a relationship, not a universal thermometer: geometry also depends on material properties, boundary conditions, water circulation, and the thermal history.

## Evidence and qualifications
- The U.S. National Park Service describes columnar jointing as regularly spaced fractures forming prismatic columns, usually with six sides but varying from three to seven. Cooling contraction creates fractures that propagate inward from the top and bottom; the hexagonal tendency is associated with stress relief at roughly 120-degree intersections. It also notes occurrence in basalt, other lava compositions, ignimbrites, domes, volcanic necks, and shallow intrusions: https://www.nps.gov/subjects/volcanoes/columnar-jointing.htm
- Goehring and Morris (2008) relate column radius and striae size to thermal and fracture models; column radius is inversely related to cooling rate in their observations: https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2007jb005018
- Experiments on Icelandic basalt found incipient macroscopic fracture between approximately 890 and 840 °C, below the solidus, showing that jointing occurs after solidification rather than while the lava remains molten: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-03842-4
- Columnar joint-like structures occur in materials and settings beyond cooling basalt. A 2019 review discusses examples involving drying and heating-related contraction, cautioning that prismatic geometry alone does not establish a cooling origin: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191814118302244

## Finding
**Supported:** Cooling-induced contraction and fracture propagation explain columnar jointing in volcanic bodies. The columns tend to be polygonal, commonly hexagonal, but are not geometrically perfect.  
**Supported with qualifications:** Column width and fracture markings can preserve information about cooling rate and thermal history.  
**Not supported:** A hexagonal or prismatic column pattern, by itself, proves that the material is basalt or that it formed by cooling lava.  
**MP contribution:** Better visibility of linked heat-flow and fracture-path dimensions, plus a useful warning against inferring origin from shape alone. No new physical mechanism and no demonstrated answer-quality improvement.
