## Question
How can geckos adhere to vertical surfaces and ceilings, then detach their feet rapidly while running?

## Conventional explanation
Gecko toe pads are hierarchical adhesive structures. The visible toe pads contain rows of microscopic hairs called **setae**; each seta branches into many much smaller terminal structures called **spatulae**. This branching arrangement creates a very large real contact area between the foot and a surface.

At the nanoscale, close contact allows weak van der Waals interactions between the spatulae and the surface to add up to substantial adhesion. The effect does not depend on sticky liquid or suction cups. Experiments with isolated setae found adhesion on both strongly hydrophobic and hydrophilic surfaces, supporting van der Waals forces rather than capillary adhesion as the primary dry-adhesion mechanism in the tested system.

The animal controls the attachment mechanically. It presses and rolls its toes into contact, aligning the tiny structures so they can generate adhesion and friction. To detach, it rolls or peels the toes away, changing the pulling angle and progressively breaking contact, much like peeling a strip of tape rather than pulling the entire surface straight off at once. This lets the gecko switch between strong attachment and low-force release quickly.

This does not mean geckos adhere equally well to every surface under all conditions. Surface roughness, contamination, water films, toe-pad morphology, and species differences matter. Wet conditions can reduce adhesion when water separates the interacting surfaces; the degree of wet performance varies.

## What the comparison shows
The conventional account already explains the physical mechanism. MP makes two aspects particularly easy to inspect:
- **Scale accumulation:** many tiny contacts combine into useful whole-foot force.
- **State change:** adhesion is not a permanent property switched on by sticky chemistry; toe orientation and peeling mechanics actively change the contact state.

These are useful organisational emphases, not new biological mechanisms. The experimental literature supplies the mechanism and the evidence.

## Evidence and qualifications
- Autumn et al. (2002), *PNAS*, provided direct experimental evidence that dry adhesion of Tokay gecko setae is consistent with van der Waals forces; their experiments argued against capillary adhesion as the primary mechanism in the tested system. https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.192252799
- Tian et al. (2006), *PNAS*, analysed the relationship between adhesion, friction and toe-controlled attachment/detachment. Rolling toes inward increases effective adhesion/friction; rolling them outward peels the spatulae away and reduces attachment. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1748224/
- A 2024 review describes the hierarchical toe-pad structure: lamellae, setae and terminal spatulae. https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/cs/d3cs00764b
- A 2019 study reviews how wet environments can disrupt dry adhesion when water films separate the surfaces, while noting variation in wet performance. https://academic.oup.com/icb/article/59/1/214/5381090
- A 2024 study of the miniature gecko *Chatogekko amazonicus* shows that pad morphology and climbing capability vary across gecko lineages and body sizes. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38803286/

## Finding
**Supported:** Gecko dry adhesion relies on hierarchical toe structures that establish extensive close contact; van der Waals forces contribute substantially to adhesion in studied species.  
**Supported:** Toe and foot motion actively regulate attachment and peeling detachment.  
**Not supported:** Gecko feet are simply sticky, use suction cups, or adhere equally well to every surface in every condition.  
**MP contribution:** Makes the accumulation of many small contacts and the controllable transition between attachment and release more explicit. No new physical mechanism or demonstrated answer-quality gain.
