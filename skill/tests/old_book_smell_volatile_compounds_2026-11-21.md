# Why Old Books Smell Different — 2026-11-21

## Test type
Normal daily test. Test ID: MP-2026-11-21-01.

## Question
Why do old books smell different from one another—sometimes sweet or vanilla-like, sometimes sharp or vinegary, sometimes musty—and what can those smells tell us about their materials and history?

## Observation
“Old book smell” is not one chemical scent. A book is a composite object: paper, sizing, ink, adhesives, binding materials and sometimes leather or other coverings. Volatile compounds released by those materials, and by changes they undergo, combine into the smell. Age alone cannot explain why two books smell different.

## Mechanisms and evidence

### Paper degradation produces a mixture of volatile compounds
Strlič et al. (2009) used headspace analysis on historic paper and correlated volatile degradation products with properties relevant to preservation, including rosin, lignin, carbonyl-group content, cellulose degree of polymerisation and paper acidity. Acetic acid and furfural were among known paper-degradation volatiles; lipid-peroxidation products were also identified as compounds of interest. The paper's composition and condition matter, not just its age.

### Sweet, vanilla-like and smoky notes
In a study of a naturally aged groundwood-pulp book, Lattuati-Derieux et al. identified 36 volatile organic compounds. Furfural, 5-methylfurfural, vanillin and guaiacol were considered relevant carbohydrate- and lignin-degradation compounds. Vanillin can contribute a vanilla-like note; guaiacol is associated with smoky/phenolic notes. These are candidate contributors in the measured book, not a universal recipe for every old book.

### Sharp or vinegary notes
Acetic acid is a documented volatile degradation product of historic paper. Acid-catalysed cellulose hydrolysis is also important to paper deterioration because it shortens cellulose chains and weakens the sheet. An acidic odour can therefore be a clue, but smell alone cannot quantify paper acidity or remaining strength.

### Mustiness and microbial compounds
A laboratory study of Aspergillus flavus cultures identified 1-octen-3-ol and cis-2-octen-1-ol among predominant volatiles and associated these compounds with characteristic musty-fungal odour. This establishes a plausible microbial source of mustiness; it does not prove that every musty-smelling book has active mould. Odour, damp-storage history, visible growth and object condition are different forms of evidence.

## Findings
Old-book smell is a composite material-history signal. Vanilla-like notes can involve vanillin; sharp notes can include acetic acid; furfural and related compounds can reflect carbohydrate degradation; and musty notes can arise from microbial volatiles. The mix varies with paper composition, binding and applied materials, previous interventions and storage conditions. Odour alone is not a reliable diagnosis of active mould or a complete conservation assessment.

## Limits and unresolved questions
- Odour descriptions are subjective and affected by the surrounding air and the listener.
- A compound detected in an ageing experiment or fungal culture is not proof that it dominates the smell of a particular book.
- The fungal study is not a test of book collections; it identifies compounds from a culture.
- The 2006 study examined one naturally aged groundwood-pulp book with accelerated-ageing treatments. Its results should not be universalised.
- A non-invasive volatile profile may help assess historic material, but conservation decisions require object-specific evidence.
