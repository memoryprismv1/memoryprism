# CAM Photosynthesis: Night-Time Carbon Capture and Day-Time Use — 2026-11-22

## Test type
Normal daily topic. Test ID: MP-2026-11-22-01.

## Question
Why do some plants take in much of their carbon dioxide at night, then use that stored carbon during the day?

## Observation and scope
Crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) is a photosynthetic pathway in which net atmospheric CO₂ uptake commonly occurs at night. The plant temporarily stores carbon in organic acids, principally malate, and later releases CO₂ internally for photosynthesis during daylight. This is not simply a nocturnal opening-and-daytime-closing valve: it is a coordinated biochemical and temporal arrangement.

## Mechanism and evidence

### The typical four-phase account
The four-phase model is a useful general description, not an inflexible timetable for every CAM species:

1. **Phase I — night:** stomata open and atmospheric CO₂ is fixed by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC). The resulting carbon is converted to malate and stored in vacuoles, principally as malic acid.
2. **Phase II — dawn transition:** stomatal behaviour and the relative contributions of PEPC and Rubisco shift. Some species show a brief period of atmospheric CO₂ uptake.
3. **Phase III — daytime:** stored malate is released and decarboxylated. The internally released CO₂ is refixed by Rubisco through the Calvin–Benson cycle, often while stomata are closed.
4. **Phase IV — late light period:** some CAM plants reopen stomata and take up atmospheric CO₂ directly, depending on conditions and the remaining malate pool.

The temporal separation allows carbon acquisition to occur when evaporative demand is often lower, while daytime photosynthesis can continue using internally supplied CO₂. CAM is therefore associated with high water-use efficiency, although the extent of the advantage and the daily pattern vary with species and environment.

### Flexibility and limits
Facultative CAM plants can increase CAM expression under conditions such as drought while using predominantly C₃ photosynthesis under more favourable conditions. CAM should not be treated as one fixed nocturnal schedule shared identically by every species. The four-phase model is a general framework; phase expression and stomatal timing are flexible.

A useful distinction is that night-time acid accumulation is a biochemical feature, not merely an observation that stomata happen to be open at night. A review surveying 40 vascular-plant families argues that nocturnal acidification is restricted to CAM species and involves metabolic reprogramming that couples stored-carbohydrate breakdown to net dark CO₂ fixation.

## Findings
The central mechanism is a time-shifted carbon pathway: atmospheric CO₂ is acquired and stored at one time, then released internally and refixed at another. Memory is materially relevant because the stored malate and carbohydrate pools connect different phases of the daily cycle. Constraints and Editors explain why the process is regulated rather than automatic.

The water-conservation explanation is important but incomplete on its own. It describes a major functional advantage; the biochemical sequence explains how the plant makes daytime carbon fixation possible while limiting daytime gas exchange.

## Limits and unresolved questions
- This is a literature-informed explanation, not a measurement of a particular plant.
- The four-phase model is a generalisation; species and environmental conditions differ.
- The water-use advantage is not quantified here and should not be assumed identical across species or habitats.
- This record does not compare the energetic cost of CAM with C₃ photosynthesis.
