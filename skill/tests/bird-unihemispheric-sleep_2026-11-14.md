# Unihemispheric sleep in birds — 2026-11-14

## Test type
Normal daily test. Topic selected independently of recent conversation topics and projects.

## Question
Why do some birds sleep with one eye open, and what does that behaviour actually tell us about their brain state?

## Observation and explanation
Some birds exhibit unihemispheric slow-wave sleep: one cerebral hemisphere shows slow-wave sleep while the other remains relatively alert. The eye associated with the more alert hemisphere may remain open, allowing the bird to monitor part of its surroundings. This can support vigilance while still permitting sleep.

The useful distinction is between:
- **Unihemispheric sleep:** the hemispheres occupy markedly different sleep/wake states.
- **Asymmetric sleep:** the hemispheres differ in sleep depth, without necessarily placing one in a fully awake state.
- **Open-eye behaviour:** an observable posture, not by itself proof of a particular EEG state.

The eye/brain relationship is contralateral. But an open eye alone cannot establish that the opposite hemisphere is fully awake; electrophysiological evidence is needed.

A 2025 study of European jackdaws reported that sleep pressure changes the balance between asymmetric and more symmetric sleep. Following sleep deprivation, birds showed less asymmetric sleep and more symmetric sleep. This is evidence that vigilance-related sleep allocation is not a fixed switch; it is influenced by accumulated sleep need.

## Critical-thinking examination
- Do not equate an open eye with a fully awake hemisphere.
- Do not treat all asymmetric sleep as strictly unihemispheric.
- Separate behavioural observation from EEG measurement.
- Ask whether the sleep pattern changes with species, context and accumulated sleep pressure.

## Findings
The phenomenon is not simply “half the brain sleeps while the other half watches.” That is a useful shorthand, but it hides important distinctions in sleep depth and measurement. The central finding is flexible allocation: birds can maintain some environmental monitoring while sleeping, and the degree of hemispheric asymmetry can shift with sleep pressure.

## Limits and unresolved questions
- The phenomenon varies across species; findings from jackdaws should not be generalised to all birds.
- Behavioural observations cannot independently establish hemispheric sleep state.
- The ecological trade-off between vigilance and restoration is plausible, but the relative contribution of predation risk, social context and sleep debt needs species- and context-specific evidence.
