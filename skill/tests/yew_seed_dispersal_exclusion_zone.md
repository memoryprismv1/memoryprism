# Test MP-2026-11-01-01 — Yew Seed Dispersal and the Hypothesised Exclusion Zone

**Date:** 2026-11-01  
**Test type:** Query-to-MP mapping audit; ecological mechanism check; response-pattern evaluation.  
**Status:** Research-backed qualitative test completed. Not a controlled blind comparison.

## 1. Test question

A prior observation proposed that a plant which discourages occupancy, but produces fleshy fruit eaten by dispersers, might create a small exclusion zone around itself: animals eat the fruit, move away, and deposit seeds elsewhere.

**Test prompt:**

> Does the poisonous yew create a tiny exclusion zone around itself through the way its fruits are eaten and its seeds dispersed? What actually determines where new yew seedlings establish?

The test deliberately separates seed dispersal from seedling establishment. A seed being moved away does not establish that it germinates or survives there.

**Mapping correction noted during the test:** frugivorous birds are primarily dispersal agents in this example. They should not automatically be labelled Editors. They qualify as editors only where their behaviour differentially affects which seeds or configurations persist. Seed predators, herbivores and microclimatic conditions are clearer post-dispersal filters.

## 3. Evidence pack

1. Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, [Taxus baccata — Plants of the World Online](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn:lsid:ipni.org:names:306036-2/general-information). Kew describes the red fleshy aril as harmless, notes that other parts of the tree are poisonous, and explains that birds eat the aril and disperse the seeds.
2. García et al. (2005), [“Spatial concordance between seed rain and seedling establishment in bird-dispersed trees: does scale matter?”](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2745.2005.01004.x), *Journal of Ecology*. In the Taxus baccata study, seed rain was greatest under canopies of the same species, and seed rain, post-dispersal predation and seedling establishment had different spatial patterns. For Taxus, seed rain was a weaker predictor at the microsite scale because post-dispersal losses varied.
3. Giertych (2000), [“Factors determining natural regeneration of yew (Taxus baccata L.) in the Kórnik Arboretum”](https://www.idpan.poznan.pl/pl/vol-455/45-31-40), *Dendrobiology*. The study reported 82.5% of regeneration under canopy, 17.3% near trunks and 0.2% in open areas. It proposed thrushes as primary dispersers to under-canopy sites and nuthatches as dispersers near mother plants; survival varied with microsite conditions.
4. García & Obeso (2003), [“Facilitation by herbivore-mediated nurse plants in a threatened tree, Taxus baccata: local effects and landscape level consistency”](https://nsojournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/j.0906-7590.2003.03601.x), *Ecography*. Yew seed rain was concentrated under yew and holly canopies; first-year seedling survival was higher beneath hollies in the studied sites.
5. Maleki et al. (2024), [“Seed Regeneration in Taxus baccata: Unveiling Ecological Restrictions and Paving the Way for Future Studies”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11554373/), *Ecology and Evolution*. Four-year monitoring identified morphophysiological dormancy, seed predation and environmental factors as likely contributors to poor regeneration. Seeds generally do not germinate until at least the second spring after dispersal, and may germinate later.
6. [“Effect of frugivorous birds on the establishment of a naturally regenerating population of Chinese yew in ex situ conservation”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21396068/). In a Chinese yew population, the nearest observed seedling was more than 10 m from mother trees, and bird dispersal was implicated in regeneration. This is a different Taxus species and should not be treated as direct proof of the same spatial pattern in T. baccata.
7. [“Seed handling by primary frugivores differentially influence post-dispersal seed removal of Chinese yew”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26846724/). Shows that bird-regurgitated and mammal-defecated seeds can have different secondary removal outcomes; primary dispersal does not guarantee successful secondary dispersal or establishment.

## 4. Ordinary explanation

The yew's red aril attracts birds. The fleshy aril is the edible part; the seed is generally swallowed and later passed or regurgitated, allowing the plant to move seeds away from the parent. The seed itself is not simply an edible package: its protective tissues and chemical properties are relevant to whether animals consume or transport it.

But this does not automatically produce an exclusion zone.

In studied populations of European yew (*Taxus baccata*), birds often deposit many seeds beneath yew canopies, not exclusively at a distance from the parent. Some seeds are moved to other trees and shrubs, where perching birds deposit them. Nuthatches can also move seeds close to mother plants.

The next stage changes the spatial pattern again. Rodents and other animals remove or eat seeds; seeds may remain dormant for years; seedlings need suitable moisture, shade and light conditions; and browsing can kill young plants. A site receiving many seeds may therefore produce few surviving seedlings.

The evidence does not support a simple ring of exclusion around yew caused by poisonous tissues and animal dispersal. It supports a more complicated, species- and scale-dependent pattern of seed movement, deposition, loss and recruitment.

## 5. MP-guided interpretation

The useful distinction is between **where seeds arrive** and **where seedlings persist**.

- Flow Under Constraint helps identify the routes and deposition locations created by bird and mammal behaviour, including perching and canopy structure.
- Constraints help identify germination requirements, dormancy, moisture, temperature and light.
- Editors are clearer in the post-dispersal stage: seed predators, browsing animals, drought and other factors alter which seeds and seedlings survive.
- Accumulation Patterns is relevant to clustered seed rain and recruitment, but a cluster of deposited seeds is not necessarily a cluster of successful seedlings.
- The Board is not just the parent tree's immediate footprint. It includes the surrounding vegetation and microhabitats that determine deposition and survival.

The important result is that the hypothesised exclusion zone cannot be inferred from the fruit's toxicity pattern alone. The spatial outcome is produced by the interaction of dispersal behaviour and post-dispersal filters.

## 7. Limitations

- Much of the cited spatial ecology concerns *Taxus baccata* in European study sites; the Chinese-yew studies are related but distinct species.
- “Exclusion zone” was a proposed pattern, not a measured effect in the cited studies.
- The cited research does not establish that toxicity evolved to create a local exclusion zone.
- The original idea also referred to plants that dislike occupancy. That property has not been operationalised or demonstrated for yew here.
- The assistant generated the mapping and explanation; no blind external assessor scored the table.

## 8. Next discriminating question

If a tree produces many seeds but few seedlings, which stage is responsible for the mismatch?

A useful field design would separately measure:
1. seed rain by distance and microhabitat;
2. seed removal and predation;
3. germination timing;
4. first-year seedling emergence;
5. survival and browsing.

This would show whether the pattern is primarily dispersal-driven, post-dispersal filtering, or a combination.

## 9. Run conclusion

**Research-backed qualitative test completed.**

The simple exclusion-zone hypothesis is not supported by the evidence reviewed. Yew seed dispersal is not a one-way process that reliably moves seeds away from the parent, and seed deposition is not equivalent to recruitment. Seeds may be deposited beneath yew canopies, other trees or shrubs; subsequent predation, dormancy, microclimate and browsing reshape the pattern.

The new pre-interpretation table is useful because it exposed a potential mapping error: dispersal agents and persistence filters have different roles. Keep the table-first response pattern; do not rename framework concepts or force all concepts into every query.
