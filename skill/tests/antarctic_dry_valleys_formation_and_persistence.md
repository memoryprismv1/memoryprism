## Question
How did the McMurdo Dry Valleys acquire their present landscape, and why do large areas remain ice-free despite being in Antarctica?

## Evidence pack
- The Transantarctic Mountains divert East Antarctic ice around the region and create a precipitation shadow. Valley-floor snowfall is very low; much snow sublimates before melting. [Obryk et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1029/2019JD032180); [Fountain et al. 2010](https://www.glaciers.pdx.edu/fountain/MyPapers/FountainEtAl2010_SnowDryValleys.pdf).
- The terrain has a complex history: fluvial processes shaped parts of the pre-glacial landscape, and glaciers subsequently incised and modified it. Contributions vary by location. [Prentice et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1029/AR072p0001); [The pre-glacial landscape of Antarctica](https://doi.org/10.1080/14702541.2018.1535090).
- Wind redistributes snow and influences sublimation; katabatic/föhn events shape local temperature and humidity. [Nylen et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1029/2003JD003937).
- Local glaciers still supply summer meltwater to ephemeral streams and closed-basin lakes. [Gooseff et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-8198.2011.00445.x); [Cross et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1029/2022JF006833).

## Condition A — ordinary baseline
The McMurdo Dry Valleys were shaped by a long sequence of fluvial and glacial processes. Rivers and streams helped establish parts of the older landscape, while repeated glacier advances and retreats carved and modified the valleys. The Transantarctic Mountains influenced how continental ice moved through the region, diverting much of the East Antarctic Ice Sheet around the valleys.

Their present ice-free character is mainly a climatic and topographic outcome. The mountains create a precipitation shadow, so valley floors receive very little snow. Strong winds redistribute snow, and much of what accumulates sublimates directly into the atmosphere before it can melt. The region is therefore extremely dry despite its freezing temperatures.

The valleys are not entirely without ice or water. Local glaciers survive, and summer meltwater flows briefly through streams into lakes, many of which remain covered by ice year-round. Conditions vary locally, and the landscape can respond to climatic changes.

### Interpretation
The landscape has two histories that should be kept distinct.

First is the **construction of the terrain**. The valleys are not simply holes left by the modern ice sheet. Their shape records older fluvial and glacial episodes, with repeated glacier advances modifying an already developed landscape. The evidence does not support one simple, uniform carving event for every valley and elevation.

Second is the **maintenance of the present ice-free condition**. The Transantarctic Mountains obstruct and redirect continental ice while creating a precipitation shadow. Very little snow reaches many valley floors. What arrives is redistributed by wind and often lost through sublimation before it can become sustained surface ice or soil moisture. The balance depends on incoming snow, redistribution, melt and sublimation.

This does not mean the system is dry everywhere. Local glaciers supply brief summer meltwater flows, and lakes retain ice and water in closed basins. These local reservoirs are compatible with an overwhelmingly dry valley-floor environment.

The strongest explanation is coupled: inherited topography controls pathways; mountain barriers shape ice flow and precipitation; wind and atmospheric dryness limit snow retention; and local energy conditions permit brief melt without widespread persistent surface ice.

### Evidence boundary
The broad mechanisms are supported, but the precise chronology and relative contribution of fluvial versus glacial shaping vary across locations and remain matters for geomorphological reconstruction. The mapping table is not an additional causal theory.
