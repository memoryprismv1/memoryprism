## Question

Why do old window panes often look uneven or wavy? Does glass slowly flow over centuries, or is the appearance inherited from manufacture?

## Evidence reviewed

- MIT School of Engineering, [How does glass change over time?](https://engineering.mit.edu/ask-an-engineer/how-does-glass-change-over-time/): old window glass does not slowly flow at room temperature; visible irregularities originate in historical manufacturing. Glass behaves as a stable solid at ordinary temperatures.
- Historic England, [Traditional Windows: Their Care, Repair and Upgrading](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/traditional-windows-care-repair-upgrading/): historical crown and cylinder processes produced characteristic surface variations.
- Scotland's Building Conservation Centre, [Glass](https://www.engineshed.scot/building-advice/building-materials/glass/): crown glass can show curved/concentric marks; cylinder glass commonly shows elongated ripples and bubbles.

The manufacturing account is the supported explanation. The exact history of an individual pane cannot be inferred from waviness alone.

## Conventional explanation

Older glassmaking methods did not produce the uniformly flat surfaces associated with modern float glass. Crown glass was made by spinning a blown glass bubble into a disc; cylinder glass was made by blowing a cylinder, splitting it, and flattening it. These processes left characteristic thickness variations, ripples, and optical distortions. The glass retains those features after cooling; it is not slowly flowing down the window.

## MP-guided explanation

The useful distinction is between the glass's present behaviour and the history that produced its shape. At manufacturing temperatures, glass can be formed by blowing, spinning, splitting, and flattening. Cooling preserves the resulting geometry. The pane's later optical waviness is therefore a record of manufacture, not evidence of continuing room-temperature flow.
