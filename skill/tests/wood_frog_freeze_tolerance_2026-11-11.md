## Question
How can a wood frog (*Rana sylvatica*) survive when much of its body water freezes, its heart stops, and it stops breathing?

## Conventional explanation
Wood frogs are freeze-tolerant vertebrates. When ice forms in contact with the skin, it triggers physiological responses that help control where ice forms and protect cells from the consequences.

A major response is rapid mobilisation of glucose from liver glycogen. The glucose is distributed through the body and acts as a cryoprotectant: it limits the amount of water drawn out of cells as extracellular ice forms, helps preserve cell volume and membranes, and reduces freezing injury. Urea and other protective adjustments also contribute. Ice is generally tolerated in extracellular spaces; uncontrolled intracellular ice would be much more damaging.

Freezing also stops circulation, breathing and detectable heart activity. The animal therefore faces not just ice formation but prolonged oxygen deprivation, dehydration/osmotic stress, and the oxidative stress associated with thawing and restored circulation. Wood frogs depress metabolic activity and adjust cellular protection and energy use so they can endure this suspended physiological state.

The often-repeated figure that 65–70% of body water can freeze refers to studied wood frogs under particular conditions; it is not a universal tolerance limit for every population or every freeze. Duration and minimum temperature also vary with population, season, acclimatisation and natural versus laboratory conditions.

An especially interesting ecological detail is that Alaskan wood frogs can undergo multiple freeze-thaw events early in winter. In field observations they remained frozen for an average of 193 days, and laboratory work suggests repeated freeze-thaw cycles can incrementally increase tissue glucose concentrations. That does not mean thawing is harmless in every circumstance: survival depends on the whole seasonal preparation and physiological context.

## What the comparison shows
The conventional explanation already identifies the main biological mechanisms. MP makes the timing and dependencies especially visible: glucose must be mobilised and distributed before circulation stops; the location of ice matters; and survival depends on a prepared seasonal state rather than one miraculous antifreeze molecule.

MP also helps distinguish the reversible frozen state from the damage that must be limited during freezing and thawing. These are organisational emphases, not new biological mechanisms.

## Evidence and qualifications
- A 2021 review reports that wood frogs can tolerate freezing of roughly 65–70% of total body water as extracellular ice, with glucose and urea cryoprotection, metabolic depression and cellular adaptations. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8067143/
- Larson et al. (2014) followed Alaskan wood frogs in natural hibernacula: frogs remained frozen for 193 ± 11 consecutive days, with 100% survival in the observed sample (N=18). Natural frogs had substantially higher tissue glucose than laboratory-frozen frogs. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24737762/
- Larson and Barnes (2016) found that successive freeze-thaw events increased glucose concentrations stepwise in measured tissues; three cycles brought levels close to those observed in naturally frozen Alaskan frogs. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27327184/
- A 2023 study describes additional cellular stress responses to freezing, anoxia and dehydration, including endoplasmic-reticulum stress and protective responses. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1355814523000792
- Freeze tolerance is seasonally variable. Alaskan winter frogs tolerate substantially colder conditions than spring frogs, associated with different cryoprotectant reserves and other physiological differences. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1155/2014/750153

## Finding
**Supported:** Wood frogs survive controlled extracellular freezing through multiple coordinated adaptations, especially glucose cryoprotection, metabolic depression and cellular protection.  
**Supported with qualifications:** Some Alaskan populations survive long natural frozen periods and repeated early-season freeze-thaw cycles; the exact limits vary with ecological and physiological context.  
**Not supported:** A single antifreeze compound explains the entire phenomenon, or every wood frog can tolerate the same temperature, ice fraction or duration.  
**MP contribution:** Makes the timing, distribution dependencies, seasonal preparation and reversible state transition more inspectable. No new biological mechanism or demonstrated answer-quality gain.
