## Question

Why do some lakes turn pink or reddish? Is the colour caused by one organism, and what makes it appear or disappear?

## Evidence reviewed

- Maldhure et al. (2023), [Identification of environmental stress parameters to study the natural colour change of water in highly saline inland Crater Lake at Lonar, India](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36995487/), *Environmental Monitoring and Assessment*. The study reports Lonar Lake's 2020 green-to-brown-to-pinkish-red change. It attributes the red appearance primarily to carotenoid production by Dunaliella under stress, with high dissolved solids, alkalinity and alkaline pH associated with evaporation and limited rainfall.
- [The microbiology of red brines](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32948267/) (2020 review): hypersaline red brines can contain several pigment-producing groups, especially halophilic archaea with bacterioruberin, beta-carotene-rich Dunaliella, and Salinibacter with salinixanthin.
- NASA Earth Observatory, [Lonar Lake Tries On a Rosy Color](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/lonar-lake-tries-on-a-rosy-color-146859/): explains the distinct contributions of halophilic microbes and Dunaliella, and discusses evaporation-driven salinity changes at Lonar and other lakes.

## Conventional explanation

Some saline lakes become pink because dense populations of salt-tolerant microorganisms contain or produce red-orange pigments. The exact organisms and pigments vary. Halophilic archaea can contain bacterioruberin; Dunaliella salina can accumulate beta-carotene; other microbes can contribute additional pigments.

Salinity and water balance help shape the ecology. Evaporation concentrates salts and changes the chemical environment; rainfall can dilute it. Light, nutrients, pH, alkalinity and other conditions influence which organisms thrive and how much pigment they contain. At Lonar, the 2020 colour change was linked to stressed Dunaliella producing carotenoids that masked its green chloroplast pigment.

So “pink lake” is a visible outcome, not a diagnosis of one species or one mechanism. Different lakes can reach similar colours through different microbial communities, and a lake's colour can change over time.

## MP-guided explanation

The colour is best treated as an outcome of an ecosystem in a particular state, not as a property of the water itself. Environmental conditions alter which organisms can grow and how they express protective pigments. Their abundance and pigments then change the light absorbed and reflected by the water.

This framing separates three things that can otherwise be collapsed: the environmental conditions, the organisms' response, and the visible colour. It also prevents the shortcut “pink means Dunaliella”: multiple microbial groups can contribute, and different lakes have different ecological histories.

## Comparison and finding

- The conventional explanation already gives the key mechanism: pigmented halophilic organisms, with salinity and other environmental conditions shaping the community and pigment expression.
- MP makes the causal chain and the distinction between environmental conditions, biological response, and visible outcome more explicit.
- MP also makes the time-varying nature of the phenomenon easier to see: a colour state can emerge and disappear as the board changes.
- The mapping adds some organisational clarity, but it does not produce a new biological mechanism or establish that the extra structure improves a straightforward answer.

**Result:** Modest explanatory organisation gain; no demonstrated content-quality gain. The important factual safeguard is that pink lakes do not all share one organism or one exact cause.
