## Starting question

How do marine mussels attach themselves to wet, wave-beaten surfaces? More specifically, how can they make an adhesive underwater, where water would normally interfere with contact between glue and a surface?

## Findings and mechanism

### 1. The mussel does not simply squirt ordinary glue onto a wet rock

Marine mussels, including species in the genus *Mytilus*, attach using byssal threads. Each thread ends in a flattened adhesive plaque that bonds to a submerged surface. The threads are collagen-rich and can stretch substantially; the plaque is the interfacial adhesive element. The system is composite: the plaque anchors, while the thread can deform and dissipate mechanical energy under wave loading.

A 2019 review describes the thread as a bundle of collagenous material with a plaque at its end. Reversible metal-coordination bonds in the thread contribute to its extensibility and shock-absorbing behaviour. The plaque contains multiple specialised mussel foot proteins, not one universal glue molecule.

### 2. The mussel controls the chemistry before the glue meets seawater

The 2026 *Nature Communications* study provides a particularly useful view of the manufacturing sequence. Plaque protein precursors are packaged in micron-scale secretory vesicles as fluid condensates and stockpiled in specialised glands in the mussel's foot. During byssus formation, these condensates are delivered through small channels to the attachment site.

The storage environment is acidic (reported around pH 3–4). Exposure to alkaline seawater (around pH 8.2) changes the chemical conditions. Salt and pH shifts drive liquid-liquid phase separation and subsequent solidification. The mussel is therefore not trying to make a finished adhesive function unchanged in seawater; it stores and delivers precursors under one set of conditions, then uses the external environment as part of the curing transition.

### 3. DOPA matters, but it is not the whole adhesive system

Much of the established research has focused on DOPA (3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine), a modified amino acid found in mussel foot proteins. DOPA-rich proteins, particularly mfp-3 and mfp-5 in the plaque, contribute to interactions at the substrate interface. Depending on the surface and local chemistry, DOPA can participate in different binding interactions. Lysine and other residues can influence those interactions.

The 2026 study identifies a previously uncharacterised histidine-rich protein, mefp-12, in *Mytilus edulis*. The authors localise it to the plaque secretory system and report that a histidine-rich peptide from it undergoes zinc- and pH-dependent phase separation, coalescence and spreading in vitro. Exposure to seawater-like pH promotes self-organisation into solid nanoporous networks resembling the native glue scaffold.

This revises a DOPA-centric explanation. DOPA-rich proteins remain important for interfacial adhesion, but the formation and curing of the porous scaffold also involve other proteins and metal-dependent assembly. A good explanation must keep those jobs distinct.

### 4. Why make a porous plaque rather than a solid lump?

The plaque has an interconnected open-cell micro- and nanoporous structure. The 2026 paper describes a solid scaffold with fluid protein condensates occupying pores. The authors suggest that the pore-held fluid phase may extend functional lifetime, while the scaffold helps dissipate energy from wave impacts. Earlier work also describes the plaque matrix as a structural foam.

This suggests a division of labour:
- interfacial proteins establish contact with the wet substrate;
- the scaffold provides cohesion and a load-bearing porous architecture;
- the collagenous thread deforms and dissipates energy;
- reversible interactions allow some mechanical energy to be absorbed without immediate catastrophic detachment.

The structure is not simply maximising the strength of one molecular bond. It is distributing different mechanical and chemical jobs across a hierarchy of components.

### 5. Competing emphases and what would distinguish them

Two incomplete explanations can be contrasted:

- **DOPA-dominant account:** adhesion performance is primarily determined by DOPA-mediated binding at the substrate. It predicts that selectively reducing DOPA-mediated interfacial interactions should strongly reduce attachment even if the plaque scaffold still forms.
- **Scaffold-and-curing account:** attachment performance also depends critically on histidine-rich protein assembly, metal coordination and formation of the porous scaffold. It predicts that disrupting mefp-12 or zinc/pH-dependent curing should impair plaque formation or mechanical performance even when DOPA-rich interfacial proteins remain present.

The 2026 study supports the second account as an important addition, not as a reason to discard DOPA. The decisive next work would compare native-like plaques with selective perturbations of mefp-12, zinc availability, pH transition and DOPA-rich proteins, measuring both plaque structure and adhesion/mechanical performance. The present record reports published work; it does not claim those tests were performed here.

## Main finding

Mussels make underwater attachment possible by controlling the production and delivery of a multi-protein adhesive system. They store protein precursors as fluid condensates in the foot, deliver them to the surface, and expose them to seawater conditions that promote phase separation and curing. DOPA-rich proteins contribute to surface adhesion, while newly characterised histidine-rich protein mefp-12 contributes to zinc-dependent self-assembly and formation of the porous scaffold. The thread then supplies a mechanically extensible connection that dissipates wave energy.

The important correction is that “mussel glue” is not one sticky chemical. It is a manufactured, chemically triggered, multi-component architecture.

## Limits and open questions

- The new mefp-12 work combines biochemical localisation and in-vitro peptide/vesicle studies. It does not establish every in-vivo contribution of the protein under natural wave conditions.
- The 2026 study concerns *Mytilus edulis*; do not silently universalise its exact protein composition or mechanism to every mussel species.
- DOPA's surface interactions depend on substrate chemistry and experimental conditions. A single bond-strength value would not describe whole-plaque performance.
- The proposed contribution of pore-held fluid condensates to longer functional lifetime remains a proposed role, not a universal quantified lifetime result.
- No energetic cost of glue production or replenishment was quantified in the sources reviewed.
- No new biological experiment, field observation or independent comparison was conducted for this record.
