## Starting question

How can thousands or millions of periodical cicadas spend 13 or 17 years underground and then emerge within a narrow seasonal window, apparently together? What synchronises the long developmental schedule, and what triggers the final emergence?

## Findings and mechanism

### 1. Two different synchronisation problems

The striking event is often described as one mystery, but it contains at least two linked problems:

- **Long-term scheduling:** why do members of a local brood mature in the same 13th or 17th year rather than emerging gradually as individuals reach adult size?
- **Short-term release:** once the cohort is developmentally ready, what brings emergence into the same spring period?

These should not be collapsed into a single temperature-trigger explanation.

### 2. Long-term development is unusually constrained

Periodical cicada nymphs live underground, usually within roughly 60 cm of the surface and commonly at depths of 20–45 cm, feeding on xylem fluid from roots. The juvenile stage lasts essentially 13 or 17 years, with rare deviations. The life cycle is therefore not simply a normal annual insect development delayed by cold weather.

A 2025 field study examined 17-year cicada nymphs aged 11–16 years. It found that the change from white to red eyes reliably marked a decision to proceed toward emergence. Almost all sampled 16-year-old nymphs had made that transition; a smaller fraction of 12-year-old nymphs had also done so. The authors' 4-year-gate hypothesis proposes that nymphs assess developmental readiness at 4n-year checkpoints (notably years 12 and 16) against a critical body-weight threshold. Those that pass the 16-year gate emerge the following spring at 17; unusually fast-growing individuals may pass the 12-year gate and emerge at 13.

This is an evidence-supported hypothesis, not a fully solved internal clock. The study's authors explicitly state that the mechanism for counting the four-year intervals remains unknown. An epigenetic cycle is discussed as a possibility, not established fact.

### 3. The final spring trigger is a separate stage

The same 2025 study discusses emergence after overwintering in response to spring soil warming, with a threshold around 18°C cited from earlier work. The developmental decision appears to have been made months earlier, before the final winter. Spring temperature is therefore better understood as a proximate release condition for already committed nymphs, not as the explanation for the entire 13- or 17-year schedule.

A 2023 thermophysical modelling paper examines how nymphs exposed to spatially correlated soil-temperature fluctuations could make near-synchronous emergence decisions despite local variation. It is a theoretical treatment of the final collective timing problem, not direct proof of the biological mechanism.

### 4. Why mass emergence may be favoured

Predator satiation is a leading evolutionary explanation. If many cicadas emerge together, predators can consume large numbers without eliminating the cohort's reproductive opportunity. Individuals emerging in small, mistimed numbers are much more vulnerable.

A 2013 study found that periodical cicada emergences appear to alter potential avian predator populations in ways associated with lower potential predation pressure at the next emergence. This supports a role for predators in shaping cycle length, although it does not by itself explain every feature of the 13- and 17-year system.

The prime-numbered cycles may reduce repeated overlap with predator population cycles and with other broods. The evolutionary explanation is not simply “prime numbers confuse predators”; the full history involves the evolution of long juvenile development, synchrony, brood structure and predator pressure. The literature continues to debate the relative contributions of these mechanisms.

### 5. Off-schedule emergence is informative

Cicadas do not follow the schedule perfectly. Studies have documented early or late stragglers and developmental plasticity, including 4-year shifts. Such individuals often emerge in numbers too small to survive predation and reproduce successfully. Their existence is important: the brood schedule is strongly canalised, not an absolutely error-free timer.

## Main finding

The mass emergence is not one giant environmental alarm clock. It is better reconstructed as a staged system:

1. Long underground development under a shared brood schedule.
2. A proposed internal developmental timing mechanism with readiness checks at multi-year gates.
3. A pre-emergence physiological transition, visible as eye-colour change, before the final winter.
4. A spring environmental release condition, including soil warming.
5. A population-level advantage to synchrony because a large emergence can overwhelm predators.

The strongest new evidence in the reviewed literature is the 2025 field test of the 4-year-gate hypothesis. It narrows the developmental timing question but does not reveal the underlying multi-year counting mechanism.

## Limits and open questions

- The 4-year-gate model is a current hypothesis supported by field observations, not a complete mechanistic account of the clock.
- The proposed epigenetic counting mechanism remains speculative.
- The approximately 18°C soil-temperature threshold is a proximate emergence cue cited from earlier work; it does not explain the 13-/17-year developmental schedule.
- Predator satiation has empirical support, but it is not a complete explanation of brood origin, prime-numbered cycles or all geographic patterns.
- The 2023 thermophysical paper is a modelling study, not a direct field demonstration of collective decision-making.
- No new biological experiment or direct observation was performed for this record.
